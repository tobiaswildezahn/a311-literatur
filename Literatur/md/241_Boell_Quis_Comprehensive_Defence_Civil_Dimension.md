# Implementing Comprehensive Defence: Three Areas to Reinforce the Civil Dimension

**Author:** Helena Quis
**Date:** 13 January 2026
**Category:** Analysis

## Introduction

Comprehensive defence requires both military and civil preparedness working together. Russia's attacks on Ukraine demonstrate that military readiness alone is insufficient — nations must develop resilient civilian structures alongside armed forces capabilities.

Germany faces a particular challenge as NATO's central logistical hub. The country must coordinate effectively across government, private sector, civil society, and military during crises. However, Germany's defence posture remains imbalanced. While military capabilities have advanced, "civil preparedness continues to lag significantly behind." This gap threatens operational effectiveness and essential functions including civil protection, energy supply, food security, and healthcare.

Recent fiscal resources approved by the Bundestag create an opportunity to address this imbalance through strategic action in three key areas.

## 1. Strengthening Strategic Direction: From Fragmented Policy to Risk Prioritisation

Germany adopted its first National Security Strategy in 2023, introducing "integrated security" concepts and identifying priority action areas. However, the strategy lacks a systematic national risk assessment that prioritises threats to core state, economic, and societal functions.

Currently, Germany's comprehensive defence remains fragmented. The "ministerial principle" — where each ministry independently manages its portfolio — combined with federalism creates a patchwork rather than coherent national planning. Civil preparedness receives considerably less strategic attention than military components.

The newly established German National Security Council (NSC) provides institutional anchoring for integrated internal and external security. At its inaugural November meeting, the Council adopted an action plan addressing hybrid threats. However, its impact depends on establishing shared risk understanding across ministries and government levels.

A national risk assessment would establish necessary foundations, followed by politically defined core security interests. This enables transparent threat identification and communication through the next National Security Strategy.

## 2. Strengthening Civil Preparedness Planning: From OPLAN DEU to Comprehensive Civil-Military Defence

The Bundeswehr developed the classified Operational Plan Germany (OPLAN DEU) — a structured planning instrument incorporating civilian support requirements and civil-military interaction. However, no comparable civilian operational framework exists.

Civil preparedness currently identifies roles and responsibilities without translating them into concrete planning assumptions for federal, state, and municipal authorities, emergency services, the Federal Agency for Technical Relief (THW), critical infrastructure operators, or civil society organisations. This creates vague capabilities, blurred responsibilities, and unaddressed planning gaps.

Civil defence has a dual function: supporting armed forces while independently maintaining critical infrastructure, supply chains, and essential state functions. OPLAN DEU addresses only the military support dimension. A coherent civilian planning framework does not exist.

This gap creates two problems. First, civilian resilience directly affects military readiness. If civil structures collapse under parallel demands, military resources become tied up in civilian tasks, weakening overall defence capabilities.

Second, civilian planning benefits the civilian sector itself. The planning process involves stakeholders, creates communication interfaces, and translates conceptual understanding into operable structures. This produces "robust civil-civil planning that specifies personnel, material, and infrastructure requirements" and ensures integration into functioning civil-military frameworks.

Only well-coordinated civilian structures understanding their own capabilities can serve as effective military counterparts during crises. Without independent planning, comprehensive defence risks militarising civilian structures.

Establishing such planning requires additional federal and state interior ministry capacity to coordinate efforts across sectors.

## 3. Fostering a Risk Culture: Communication and Empowering Society

Civil preparedness depends on resilient structures and human factors. An informed and engaged society responds effectively to crises.

Germany currently lacks developed risk culture promoting open dialogue, shared responsibility, and critical skill development. While war, sabotage, and hybrid attacks receive increasing discussion, their implications remain unclearly communicated. Consequently, challenges remain abstract, government support limits unstated, and individual responsibilities poorly understood.

Recent initiatives — the introduction of the term *Kriegstuechtigkeit* (readiness for war) and the Federal Office of Civil Protection preparedness guide — provided important impetus. Yet efforts remain fragmented. Systematic, comprehensible, realistic risk communication across all government levels is absent.

Effective communication must inform citizens, companies, municipalities, and institutions about threat environments, state support limitations, and individual action scope. This sense of agency constitutes core societal resilience — confronting risks proactively rather than passively.

Approximately 1.7 million Germans already volunteer in civil protection. Surveys indicate younger generations show increased willingness assuming security responsibility. This potential requires active cultivation.

While conscription represents one element, targeted strengthening of existing civil-society structures proves equally important. Weekend training courses, self-protection programmes, and establishing federal civil defence associations modeled on reservist organisations could transform comprehensive defence into a genuine whole-of-society endeavour grounded in solidarity and broad participation.

## Conclusion

Germany faces mounting security challenges — military, infrastructural, and societal. Its defence posture remains incomplete without equal civilian dimension strengthening. Concepts exist, institutional foundations are established, and financial resources are available. Implementation remains lacking.

Resilient comprehensive defence emerges from strategic prioritisation, clear operational planning, and an informed, prepared, engaged society. This demands shifting political and administrative thinking toward shared risk understanding, cross-institutional responsibility, and capacity for action across federal boundaries.
