# Wrong and Useful: Models and Metaphors for Strategy

Colonel (retired) Arthur F. Lykke was a faculty instructor in the Department of National Security and Strategy at the U.S. Army War College in the late 1980s. Nearly 35 years ago, he introduced the national security world to three concepts: END, WAYS, and MEANS. Lykke passed away in late November 2021. The War Room staff presents this re-release of an earlier discussion of his influential work.

## The Core Question

What good is a strategic model when every model is clearly and manifestly wrong? War represents one of humanity's most complex endeavors. In this context, what utility do necessary simplifications provide? Perhaps strategists should acknowledge and address complexity more directly, thinking creatively about problems while avoiding demonstrably flawed models.

However, statistician George Box famously observed that "all models are wrong; some models are useful." No strategic model perfectly represents war, but some—particularly those providing systematic thinking frameworks or creating memorable communication tools—offer valuable insights justifying their limitations. Which models prove most powerful? What consequences follow from choosing one model over another? These questions form the basis of ongoing debates about strategic thinking.

Major M.L. Cavanaugh's Modern War Institute essay criticized the "ends-ways-means" model (the "Lykke model"), arguing it has been misused beyond salvaging and should be abandoned. While Cavanaugh identifies legitimate flaws, he demands too much from the model.

## Models and Language

Metaphors and models, even flawed ones, remain central to strategic thinking and instruction. At their best, models serve to both order and disorder our thinking. War colleges face substantial challenges educating future strategic leaders. Thin caricatures of current approaches solve little. Strategists must become comfortable operating across multiple metaphors and cognitive contexts, learning to harness language and imagery while avoiding captivity to any single framework.

Language shapes thinking profoundly. Analogies and metaphors form the core of cognitive function. Lakoff and Johnson explain that conceptual systems are "fundamentally metaphorical" — we understand novel concepts through familiar ones.

War and strategy employ abundant metaphors. Clausewitz masterfully used metaphorical language, describing war as duel, chameleon, commerce, politics, trinity, magnet, science, art, or card game. We readily accept this multiplicity without demanding single metaphors carry entire concepts.

Strategy, equally abstract and complex, requires concrete images. Lykke's 1989 work offered two metaphors: equation and stool. Others proposed different frameworks: strategy as bridge, wrestling match, dialogue, negotiation, or process. Cavanaugh himself recognizes single metaphors cannot bear all weight, previously criticizing Colin Gray's bridge metaphor while proposing Pike's Peak Marathon (modified with violent competition).

## The Problem of Oversimplification

Cavanaugh rightfully criticizes stool and equation metaphors as flawed. Leaders relying excessively on them artificially restrict their thinking, weakening resulting strategies. Yet common frameworks provide mutual understanding starting points. Judicious metaphor use aids central communication tasks.

The ends-ways-means construct omits important considerations regarding adversaries, environment, and adaptation to uncertainty and contingency. Deficient strategies result from ignoring these elements. However, ends-ways-means does not preclude other valuable approaches and provides unique perspectives justifying its continued use.

When strategists fail distinguishing ends from ways from means, they risk mistaking means for ends, ignoring critical causal mechanisms. This constitutes a major critique of recent National Security Strategies, which proved heavy on desired outcomes and available resources but inadequate at describing relationships between them.

Even critics find ends, ways, and means central to most strategy definitions. Barry Posen's definition — "a grand strategy is a nation-state's theory about how to produce security for itself" — contains clear hints of ends (security) and ways articulation, making means consideration inevitable.

## Moving Between Metaphors

Rather than dismissing flawed metaphors, strategists should move comfortably between them. Taking the stool metaphor, one might stretch it creatively: Are means legs the only ones risking shortness? What happens with wobbly or broken legs? What sits atop the strategy stool? What risks emerge if it breaks? Could one envision stools on concrete versus muddy fields? Could risk function as stabilizing shim? How might one improve the stool while sitting?

Conversely, applying ends-ways-means to competitive marathons on rugged terrain reveals utility. One asks about ends: winning, placing, or beating rivals? About means: sufficient water, correct equipment, adequate training? About ways linking ends and means: starting pace, psychological tactics versus physical reliance?

Play with metaphors. Stretch them. Discover their limits. Invent new ones. But avoid discarding metaphorical babies with metaphorical bathwater.

## War College Responsibilities

Cavanaugh primarily blames the U.S. Army War College for myopic, formulaic thinking, insinuating flawed models perhaps drove American strategic deficiencies in recent decades. This represents a logical error — affirming the consequent. Good war colleges may prove necessary but certainly insufficient. Cavanaugh ignores multiple explanations for strategic deficiencies: commissioning sources, service cultures, domestic politics, civil-military relations, the international system, and other factors.

The U.S. Army War College has actively challenged simplistic model usage and hosts robust debate. Lykke's influential article no longer receives assigned reading status, though its development and insights receive extensive discussion. Jeffrey Meiser's essay critiquing the Lykke model appeared in Parameters, the War College's premier journal, accompanied by substantive follow-on debate.

## Current Practice

The U.S. Army War College provides this strategy definition: "Strategy is the alignment of ends (aims, objectives), ways (concepts), and means (resources) — informed by risk — to attain goals." This differs significantly from simple "balancing" notions. The college discusses the Lykke stool model, introduces a strategy formulation framework, and teaches a suitability-acceptability-feasibility-risk (SAF-R) test for evaluating strategy. These serve as starting points and legitimate objects for criticism, discussion, and analysis — not sacred doctrine.
