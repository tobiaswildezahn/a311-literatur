# Enhancing EU-NATO Cooperation in Preparedness and Critical Infrastructure Protection
*Annegret Bendiek, Mika Kerttunen*

#### Contents
*The recent sabotage incidents in the Baltic Sea region have drawn attention to the protection of critical infrastructures. The EU-NATO cooperation typically focussing on dialogues and information exchanges should make use of the recent EU strategies updated infrastructure protection regulations. Enhancing situational awareness remains a core issue for timely political decision-making and tailored preventive, protective and responsive measures. Accordingly, Member State and subregional practices and initiatives to improve resilience, protection and preparedness should be welcomed as manifestations of EU-NATO cooperation.*

## Critical Information Infrastructure under Attack
Attacks against critical infrastructure are common and diversifying requiring enhanced European and Member-State cooperation.<sup>1</sup> The Russian war against Ukraine and the recent critical infrastructure disruptions at the Baltic Sea and elsewhere in Europe have made European governments, the EU Commission, NATO and the private sector better aware of the challenge and improve their preparedness and cooperation.

Although institutionalised cooperation between the EU and NATO in the area of defence dates back to 2005, the protection of critical infrastructures has only been comprehensively understood and implemented for the last three years. Since 2022 EU-NATO cooperation for critical infrastructure protection has been institutionalised along five pathways:

- i) the EU-NATO institutional frameworks of cooperation,
- ii) the EU strategies, directives and regulations enhancing Union and Member State postures,
- iii) NATO contingency planning and capability development with primarily defence and often cross-border focus, and
- iv) nation state local and sub-regional activities.

Especially the latter is most reactive to the challenges the operational environment, or: domain, here: critical infrastructure protection, may bring.

After several years of cooperation, there is still a lack of implementable operationalisation of cybersecurity and critical infrastructure protection. Given that a large-scale cyberattack affecting Union civilian infrastructure may activate NATO response mechanisms, th[e EU has called for](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/cyber-blueprint-draft-council-recommendation) enhanced structured cooperation with NATO and also between civilian and military actors.<sup>2</sup>

In th[e Russian war against Ukraine](https://www.swp-berlin.org/10.18449/2023C23/) as well as in the Gaza conflict critical information infrastructure has been deliberately destroyed but perhaps surprisingly rather by conventional military means, bombs, rockets and missiles than cyber means.<sup>3</sup> Yet destructive cyberattacks against critical information infrastructure have become frequent also in peacetime.

#### The institutional frameworks of EU-NATO cooperation
Institutionalized in 2000 and anchored in the [EU Strategic Compass](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/strategic_compass_en3_web.pdf)<sup>10</sup> and th[e NATO](https://www.act.nato.int/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/290622-strategic-concept.pdf) [Strategic Concept,](https://www.act.nato.int/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/290622-strategic-concept.pdf) <sup>11</sup> the [EU-NATO cooperation](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-nato-cooperation/) has focussed among others on countering hybrid threats, cyber security and defence, capacity building, and coordinated resilience and preparedness exercises.<sup>12</sup> The June 2024 [Ninth progress report](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-10471-2024-INIT/en/pdf) on the implementation of the seventy-four proposals endorsed by EU and NATO Councils in 2016 and 2017 highlights the materialized cooperation;<sup>13</sup> however, the operative expressions of actual cooperation being often political dialogue, consultations, and exchanges of information have led to [calls for](https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/news_232734.htm?selectedLocale=en) enhanced cooperation and presumably less conversation and a little more action.<sup>14</sup>

### The EU critical infrastructure protection regime
For the EU, critical infrastructure is not only closely related to policies and legislation ensuring the functioning of the internal market of the EU, but also to its security and defence agenda, including the [strategic priority](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/eu-strategic-agenda-2019-2024/) of the protection of the Union and its citizens and the EU's freedom of action. Critical infrastructure is needed for the provision of essential public services and economic activities in the internal market, as well as for security and defence.<sup>18</sup>

Over a decade, experts have been demanding for a stronger coherence and coordination of measures taken in individual sectors. Yet, inter-institutional competition continues to stand in the way of an integrated approach between the military and civilian actors in European security, crises management cycle and the protection of critical infrastructure. In the EU, there are now sector-specific agencies tasked for the protection of critical infrastructure protection such as [ENISA,](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/) [EU LISA,](https://www.eulisa.europa.eu/) [EDA,](https://eda.europa.eu/) an[d EU INTCEN](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/meetdocs/2014_2019/documents/sede/dv/sede160615factsheetintcen_/sede160615factsheetintcen_en.pdf) only to mention as few of them, which are mandated to coordinate member state policies and international partnerships for the protection of critical infrastructures more closely. The EU's central weakness remains the lack of focused identification, joint assessment and proactive, forwardlooking foreign policy responses to threats. The Member States' threat analyses and security political situation assessments diverge preventing common understanding of the threat level and coherent formulation of security policy measures. This basic problem is not new, but it has become radically acute today. For a few years now, the EU institutions have been trying to reflect the idea of comprehensive and integrated security by endeavouring to use situational awareness and preparedness as an overarching idea.

The Council tasked the European External Action Service in 2020 to provide [360](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2020_11_20_memo_questions_and_answers_-_threat_analsysis_-_copy.pdf) [degrees analysis](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2020_11_20_memo_questions_and_answers_-_threat_analsysis_-_copy.pdf) of the full range of threats and challenges.<sup>19</sup> Based on a comprehensive situational awareness, objectives are to be derived from as to how the EU can make security and defence policy investments over the next five to ten years to protect the EU and engage partners for this purpose. The findings were taken into the March 2022 Strategic Compass, the first document after the Russia's invasion in Ukraine that mentioned explicitly the need for a regular threat analysis as a guiding document for the EU's foreign, security and defence policy.<sup>20</sup> Th[e Strategic Compass](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-7371-2022-INIT/en/pdf) had a major shortcoming: it understands security in the legal framework of CSDP only. Critical organisations must assess threats, implement safeguards, and report major incidents.<sup>24</sup> For the first time, the EU sets criteria to identify organisations of European importance, whose failure could affect multiple countries.

The simultaneous adoption of the NIS2 and the CER directives in 2022 reflected rising risks, including the sabotage of the Nord Stream pipelines and Russia's war against Ukraine.

#### The new ambition in situational awareness and preparedness
Th[e Niinistö Report](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/defence/safer-together-path-towards-fully-prepared-union_en) of October 2024 on Europe's civilian and military preparedness and readiness complements the European critical infrastructure protection regime with a broader view on Union, Member-State and societal preparedness. The Report speaks how preparedness "requires a high level of trust between public authorities, Member States, EU institutions, the private sector, and civil society." Against such evolving threats as the sabotage of critical infrastructure and cyberattacks, the Report calls for enhanced private sector resilience, coordinated maritime presence, and the development of innovative capabilities to facilitate the use of Common Security and Defence Policy (CSDP) instruments in complementarity of internal security tools.<sup>27</sup>

Rather than advocating for a major overhaul of existing security structures or transferring more power to Brussels, the report emphasizes enhancing cooperation among member states and improving current mechanisms. Recommendations include strengthening the EU's disaster response capabilities, such as the Emergency Response Coordination Centre, and fostering better intelligence-sharing. Across nine thematic blocs, the report presents an exhaustive list of recommendations – such as enhancing EU-NATO cooperation, strengthening the Commission's Emergency Response Coordination Centre, and rationalizing the European defence market –, many of which the EU institutions have already been working on.

With the new EU Commission under the leadership of Ursula von der Leyen, the EU is now attempting to put the many past initiatives on critical infrastructure protection on a new footing by focusing on situational awareness and preparedness in order to finally put the idea of integrated security into practice. Explicitly building on the Niinistö Report, the March 2025 EU [Preparedness Union Strategy](https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/circabc-ewpp/d/d/workspace/SpacesStore/b81316ab-a513-49a1-b520-b6a6e0de6986/file.bin) introduces an integrated all-hazards approach, a whole-of-government approach, bringing together all relevant public authorities across local, regional, national, and EU levels of government, and a whole-of-society approach, bringing together citizens, local communities and civil society, businesses and social partners as well as the scientific and academic communities. 28

Moreover, the Preparedness Union Strategy identifies 30 key actions in a detailed [Action Plan](https://webgate.ec.europa.eu/circabc-ewpp/d/d/workspace/SpacesStore/18537b82-f73b-46ff-b1bb-4c7e02d4404a/file.bin), and seeks to develop a 'preparedness by design culture' across all EU policies. These include developing EU comprehensive risks and threats assessment and strengthening the Emergency Response Coordination Centre. The Strategy analyses how the individual areas of early warning in the EU and crisis management, particularly in civilmilitary cooperation, have so far only interacted with each other to a limited extent.<sup>29</sup>

# NATO Imperatives on preparedness and critical infrastructure protection
Th[e North Atlantic Alliance](https://www.act.nato.int/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/290622-strategic-concept.pdf) is a defence and defensive alliance. Its prerogatives are the ability to deter and defend; accordingly, its core tasks are deterrence and defence; crisis prevention and management; and cooperative security.<sup>35</sup> Without supranational and legislative powers the Alliance remains action oriented. Accordingly, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization interest i[n resilience,](https://www.nato.int/cps/en/natohq/official_texts_133180.htm) critical infrastructure protection as well as situational awareness is primarily instrumental: how do these functions and capabilities support military preparedness and operations? For example, [NATO treats](https://www.nato.int/cps/uk/natohq/topics_132722.htm?selectedLocale=en) civil preparedness as a central pillar of Allies' resilience and a critical enabler for the Alliance's collective defence, including credible deterrence and defence. NATO goes to read resilience rooted in the [Washington Treaty](https://www.nato.int/nato_static_fl2014/assets/pdf/stock_publications/20120822_nato_treaty_en_light_2009.pdf) Article 3 norm of maintaining and developing the Parties "capacity to resist armed attack".<sup>36</sup>

#### Outlook
The contemporary geopolitical contestation has made many Member States and Allies tightrope artists. While trying to preserve the European values enshrined for decades in the EU Treaties, we are careful not to irritate the United States, the Trump administration, too much. While under political and economic pressure, information campaigns, and virtual and mechanic attacks we try to maintain our political rights and civil liberties. While adhering to our regional and transatlantic political, economic and security arrangements we tailor and place our faith also on subregional mechanisms.

The recent antagonism between the Trump administration and Europe, writ large, is a part of a wider contestation over the world order authoritarian states and politicians have accelerated. Liberal values and societies are under attack. In these manoeuvres critical infrastructure represents and supports our chosen way of life. Because of these diplomatic, military, trade and economic and informational manoeuvres, societal and regional preparedness, and the subsequent resilience, are of utmost importance.

Fostering EU-NATO cooperation has its direct value in e.g. enhanced situational awareness but perhaps more importantly indirect value in maintaining functional transatlantic relations. The on-going political, administrative, and military responses and effort to improve resilience and preparedness showcase scalable and importable ways of international, inter-agency and institutional cooperation.

The approach a group of Allies (also EU Member States) outlined to enhance critical infrastructure protection at the Baltic Sea in January 2025 provides an applicable blueprint for pragmatic inter-national and inter-institutional cooperation. Member State activities and collaboration can alig[n EU and NATO ambitions](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-nato-cooperation/) beyond political dialogue, exchanges of information and intelligence and coordination of capability development and capacity building.

A pragmatic subregional bottom-up approach can help to bypass some of the longlasting political obstacles of enhanced EU-NATO cooperation. Implemented by nation states within national jurisdictions, measures will be assessed by their effects, not by their institutions. Then again, various [NATO](https://www.act.nato.int/about/centres-of-excellence/)<sup>55</sup> and [EU](https://cbrn-risk-mitigation.network.europa.eu/eu-cbrn-centres-excellence_en)<sup>56</sup> centres of excellence brin[g Member](https://www.hybridcoe.fi/) [States and Allies](https://www.hybridcoe.fi/) alike to cooperate in non-operative but facilitating settings.<sup>57</sup> To mitigate political sensitivities, cooperation needs to provide or spin identifiable benefits for all Allies and Members.

On the other hand, we underline that the strengthened European critical infrastructure protection regime together with an institutional interest on preparedness and civilmilitary cooperation could be used to boost EU-NATO cooperation in the field. The NIS2 and CER directives provide state-of-the art administrative and technical guidance which also the Allies outside of the EU could make use of. In specific, to improve EU-NATO cooperation for the all-European preparedness and critical infrastructure protection we recommend the following:

- (1) Include preparedness in the EU-NATO cooperation frameworks: The Preparedness Union Strategy sets a golden standard for preparedness, which should be applied also in EU-NATO cooperative preparedness considerations. Accordingly, more operational NATO measures could have a similar exemplary value.
- (2) Operationalize EU-NATO cooperation in preparedness, critical infrastructure protection and situational awareness: EU-NATO cooperation to proceed from words to deeds requires deliberate operationalization of the very cooperation. Such tangible measures could inter alia include the identification of dualpurpose functions and critical infrastructure, and the harmonization of the development of situational awareness capabilities. Despite differing mandates, orientation and membership, serious consideration should be paid to developing joint or dual-purpose situational awareness and undersea monitoring capabilities.
- (3) Incorporate success criteria and evaluation in strategies and reporting: Contemporary strategies and reports are input focussed. Parliaments and taxpayers deserve proper examination of the return of both political and financial investments. In addition to current accounts of dialogues, meetings and engagements, analysis on output and outcome factors such as improved measures and results, taken measures and their impact, reduced incidents or other disturbances, and more effective ways to allocate and use common resources would be appropriate.
- (4) Make agreements between stakeholders: To ensure sustainable and concrete action, politically and/or legally binding agreements from Memorandums of Understandings to contracts between stakeholder should be made.
- (5) Expand the exercise regime: If and when European situational awareness, shared understanding and timely decision making are below par, a regime of preparedness exercises should be developed for the European top leaders and the relevant EU and NATO bodies. A suitable framework for this could be the Plan for Implementation of Parallel and Coordinated Exercises (PACE).
