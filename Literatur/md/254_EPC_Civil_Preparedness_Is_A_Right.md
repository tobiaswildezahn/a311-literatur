# Civil Preparedness is a Right

**Publication Date:** January 14, 2025
**Author:** Maria Martisiute, Policy Analyst at the European Policy Centre
**Publication Type:** Commentary

## Executive Summary

This commentary argues that civil preparedness should be recognized and enshrined as a fundamental right for all European citizens. As Europe faces escalating security threats — including Russia's war on Ukraine, infrastructure attacks, cyberattacks, and other transnational crises — institutions alone cannot guarantee citizen protection. The author contends that informed and equipped populations form the backbone of societal resilience during crises.

## Key Arguments

**1. Deteriorating Security Landscape**
NATO and national security authorities estimate potential Russian military action by 2027-2029. Europe currently faces an "undeclared war with Russia on multiple fronts," including hybrid warfare, infrastructure sabotage, and disinformation campaigns.

**2. Institutional Limitations**
Despite progress in resilience-building, "national authorities, the EU and NATO are struggling to deter and prevent attacks," making citizen involvement essential for institutional defense and continuity.

**3. Direct Threat to Citizens**
Beyond infrastructure, Russian hybrid attacks pose realistic threats of "substantial casualties or very substantial economic damage" to European populations. Citizens require practical knowledge about protective measures.

**4. Inadequate Preparedness Across Europe**
Several EU and NATO members resist civil preparedness initiatives. Belgium's Justice Minister characterized population emergency preparation as "premature," while German leadership communication with Russian leadership undermines collective defense efforts.

## Policy Recommendations

1. **Enshrine civil preparedness as a basic right** in national, European, and international law alongside existing freedoms
2. **Increase investment** through civil-military defense expenditures, with dedicated funding in the 2028-2034 Multiannual Financial Framework
3. **Engage transparent dialogue** with citizens about specific threats, defense measures, and required actions at all governance levels
4. **Publish accessible preparedness guides** explaining citizen rights and duties during emergencies, including worst-case scenarios
5. **Build practical security awareness** through household and workplace engagement, emphasizing survival skills (e.g., 72-hour self-sufficiency)
6. **Support military-coordinated training** simulating crisis and conflict scenarios with lessons from Ukraine
7. **Integrate preparedness education** into school curricula to develop understanding and critical thinking

8. **Customize guidance** for vulnerable populations including elderly, disabled, hospitalized, children, pregnant women, single parents, and migrants

## Conclusion

The author frames civil preparedness as both a necessity and a right: "Civil preparedness is not a privilege – it is a civil and military necessity." Building European resilience requires leaders and citizens to establish mutual trust regarding genuine security threats, however uncomfortable this may be.
