**Description**

NATO has defined 7 baseline requirements for national resilience to help countries to prepare for serious shocks as a result of attack, terrorism, or natural disaster. These focus on continuity of government, essential services, critical infrastructure, civil communications, and the movement of people and goods across borders. Together they form the core capacities deemed necessary for a state to function under stress.

This book, The Eighth Baseline Requirement: Resilient Civilians in Hybrid Threats and Warfare is the result of the NATO SPS Multi-Year Project on Resilient Civilians in Hybrid and Population-Centric Warfare and forms part of the NATO Science for Peace and Security Series: Human and Societal Dynamics. Hostile actors increasingly target the trust, cohesion, and psychological resilience of civilian populations. Disinformation campaigns and other non-military tactics deliberately exploit social fault lines, ultimately undermining the effectiveness of the other seven baseline requirements. The book argues that investing in the promotion of public trust, media literacy, and community cohesion and officially designating resilient civilians as the eighth baseline requirement for national resilience, would acknowledge that resilience is not just about impregnable infrastructure and secure supply chains, but also about the resolve, knowledge, and cohesion of the people.

The book is divided into 2 sections, Country Case Studies and Resilience Strategies, each containing 5 chapters. There is also an Introductory chapter, a Conclusion, and a Foreword contributed by Jamie Shea, Former Deputy Assistant Secretary General of NATO for Emerging Security Challenges.

The book discusses the need for more creative approaches to resilience in the light of increasing hybrid threats, and will be of great interest to all those working to ensure national security.

**Preface**

Resilience has become a buzzword among policymakers and students of strategy alike, similar to the prevalence of concepts such as deterrence, collective defence or civil emergency planning during the Cold War. The term Resilience suggests many things to many people. For some it is the ability of societies to come together and express sentiments of solidarity and common values, normally in the wake of major terrorist attacks, political assassinations, pandemics such as the recent global Covid 19 outbreak or natural disasters, particularly the growing number of forest fires, floods or storms driven by climate change. These can impact not just small groups of people or individual cities but entire populations, causing mass displacements and casualties; or, as in the case of small island states hit by hurricanes, even prolonged societal and economic collapse. Hyperconnected infrastructure such as power grids and pipelines also mean that a problem in one area can take down an entire network across international borders as we saw recently in the electricity blackout across Spain, Portugal and southern France.

For others, Resilience is more the response to state-directed attempts to disrupt and weaken competitors and adversaries that authoritarian regimes, looking to achieve the effects of war without actually going to war, increasingly use in the present day and age. Western democracies, with their open media spaces, multinational infrastructure connections, digital networks but also increasingly polarised societies are particularly vulnerable to this form of political manipulation which NATO experts have dubbed “hybrid warfare”. The advantage of this type of warfare below the threshold of conventional military aggression is that it is relatively cheap and easy to organize and attackers can jump from one target in one country to another, as new vulnerabilities to exploit are discovered and new attack vectors are created. The victims are often unsure of the precise source of the attack, given that perpetrators can easily hide their tracks or use proxies to give themselves deniability. In turn the level of damage suffered, while significant, is unlikely to lead the victim to escalate in responding due to the risk of further attacks or the outbreak of unwanted hostilities. Hybrid warfare is thus relatively low risk and high impact. Particularly when in a NATO alliance of 32 member states only one ally might be affected while life continues as normal for all the others. Thus the Article V collective defence clause in the NATO treaty is unlikely to be invoked. Unless of course the attack reaches the level of the equivalent of an armed aggression and is clearly aimed at the alliance as a whole, as was the case with the 9/11 terrorist strikes on New York and Washington back in 2001.

Europeans have recently witnessed the full panoply of hybrid warfare tactics at work. For, contrary to conventional warfare, which is still comparatively rare and takes place in set phases of war and peace, hybrid disruptions can affect multiple targets in multiple countries simultaneously and practically on a daily basis. We have seen pipelines sabotaged and telecoms cables severed under the Baltic Sea, not by warships but by a “shadow fleet” of oil tankers of uncertain ownership. The locks of a hydroelectric dam in Norway were opened by a cyber attack. Civilian aviation across Eastern Europe and the Baltic region faces the daily nuisance of GPS navigation signals being jammed, putting passenger safety at risk. NATO member states across Europe have reported sabotage attacks on commercial centres, interference with water supplies and mysterious drones flying over airbases. Belarus has tried to push illegal migrants into Poland, and Russia has done the same to Finland, causing the latter to build a border fence. Meanwhile, Russia uses its state-funded broadcasting network, as well as armies of bots and trolls to overwhelm the social media (which western consumers are increasingly addicted to) with Russian government disinformation, fakes and narratives, even going so far as to covertly interfere in national elections. Russian intelligence operatives have carried out a number of political assassinations (or attempts) and even used potentially catastrophic nerve agents such as polonium and Novichok which could cause multiple deaths and require extensive decontamination efforts. The war in Ukraine has also had an effect, not only in pushing 6.5 million Ukrainians to move westward into Europe in search of shelter and safety, but also in the threat posed by Russian missiles and drones flying off course and crashing into NATO territory. So far and mercifully just two Polish citizens have been killed by this falling debris but with the end of the war in Ukraine nowhere in sight, the risk of a far more deadly mishap is always there.

The mixture of natural disasters or “Act of God” events and state-driven hybrid warfare campaigns has changed the nature of security and defence strategy in that it is today as much focused on the protection of the home front and of civilian populations as on the protection of borders and territory. Historians may argue that there is not anything really new here. During the World Wars of the 20th century the belligerents had to build air raid shelters to keep people safe during bombing. Home guards of civilians not deemed fit for actual military service were created to defend against foreign invasion. Others became air raid wardens or joined fire brigades. Moreover civilians, especially women, were mobilised to support the war effort, working in armaments factories, working as nurses and paramedics and driving ambulances; and those with a scientific bent of mind became code breakers and intelligence operatives. But the difference then was that civilian mobilisation only happened after war had been declared. The home front was largely an extension of the war front. By contrast, what is happening today is that the home front has become a domain of conflict in its own right and is being fought over permanently and in peace time. Moreover not just government institutions but every aspect of normal civilian life is affected. Civilians can be the unintended target of hybrid attacks as with a British woman poisoned by the Russian Novichok agent in Salisbury. They are the victims of aggressive cyber attacks as when the WannaCry malware forced the cancellation of thousands of operations in the UK National Health Service. The same also when citizens in Estonia lost access to their data and government and banking services in the wake of the cyber attack against Estonia in 2007. This would be even more the case if deliberate GPS jamming causes a civilian aircraft to crash, or a stray Russian drone or missile lands on a busy supermarket in Poland or Romania. Business is also impacted as cyber attacks shut down business operations in car production, airlines and train services or retail outlets for weeks on end, causing billions in financial (not to speak of reputational) damage. In a world in which anything and anyone can be attacked anywhere and at anytime, governments now have the permanent headache of protecting not just a border here or there, or an important military base or vital nuclear power plant but the whole of society and the whole population all at once against a multitude of risks and threats. In the daily churn of government and with limited resources, tracking the evolving threat landscape and figuring out from one day to the next what is the most urgent threat to focus on and prepare for is increasingly difficult and often guesswork. Is it sabotage, disinformation, cyber attacks, drone and missile defence, terrorism, the next pandemic or something else? The speed, simultaneity and diversity of threats to the home front as a distinct theatre of conflict are in their own right what is new.

At the same time, the home front has also become reconnected to the more traditional front of borders and territorial defence as war in Europe has unfortunately no longer become unthinkable. Many European politicians and military commanders (several of whom are not known for indulging in hype or hyperbole) have warned of the prospect of a war with Russia in as little as 5 years from now. Europe has not known a major war on its continent since the end of World War II 80 years ago. But Putin’s aggression against Ukraine has been an unpleasant but salutary reminder of what actual war really means. Thus far the Russian and Ukrainian armies fighting each other in eastern Ukraine have suffered a million casualties, millions are displaced within and outside Ukraine, thousands of homes destroyed, agriculture and the economy devastated, the energy grid almost destroyed and a population experiencing trauma under daily bombardment that it will take years to recover from. Yet Ukraine is also an example of what a determined population can achieve against a larger and more powerful aggressor when it puts its skills and capacity for innovation behind the war effort. Seeing the destruction that Russia has inflicted on Ukraine, leaders and public opinion in the NATO member states ask themselves: “Could this happen to us too”? Or, how can we be better prepared to make sure that we too can survive and fight back? And recover quickly from a conflict and rebuild when it is over? In sum, NATO governments now face a double challenge on an unprecedented scale: to protect their societies and populations against the daily threat of shocks, disruption and hybrid campaigns while at the same time preparing them for the even greater dangers of actual warfare across Europe, should Russia decide to escalate from non-military warfare to the military type itself.

One clear starting point in this process of preparation and putting Europe once more on a footing to be able to fight wars is that resilient societies require resilient citizens. Governments and official authorities cannot provide the necessary degree of preparedness and responsiveness all by themselves. This is usefully the starting point too of the present study, which recommends, and in my view rightly, that NATO should add an 8th baseline requirement to its current list of resilience targets. Back in the 1960s, President John F. Kennedy famously proclaimed: “Do not ask what your country can do for you, but what you can do for your country”. Similarly today anxious citizens will obviously look to their governments to enhance their protection. State authorities do so by introducing regulations and laws to improve security, particularly in the digital realm, by building more shelters and civil protection facilities (as in Poland and Germany), by improving early warning and government crisis communications (as in Lithuania) and by improving border controls and defences against both hybrid activities (such as mass migration) as well as armed attack as with the Baltic Shield and Eastern Shield projects. Yet to use the terminology of the French Revolution, democracies are based on “active citizens” – rather than “passive citizens” – who complement the efforts of government by making their own contributions. In recent years we have witnessed the myriad forms that civilian engagement can take. Cyber experts (including those from the hacking community) have come to the rescue of governments and companies in finding network vulnerabilities and responding to cyber attacks. NGOs have been adept at exposing Russian (and other) disinformation campaigns and using data mining to identify the perpetrators of intelligence, sabotage and assassination operations. Many European citizens have welcomed Ukrainian refugees into their homes or manned charities on a voluntary basis that have collected and transported clothing and supplies to beleaguered Ukrainian cities. Other civilians have become involved in civil defence or joined reserve forces undergoing military training to be ready in the event of disasters or war. Eleven NATO countries have conscription and others, like Germany, are actively considering its re-introduction if voluntary schemes to increase the size of armed forces do not produce satisfactory results. Other countries, for instance Sweden, Norway and Finland, have long practiced the whole-of-society approach in which governments, local authorities, private sector and citizens groups consult together regularly to ensure that they will have a coherent and joined up approach in crisis situations. Companies allow their employees to participate in the training programmes and reserve organisations. The old World War II poster asking: “What did you do in the war, Daddy?” today potentially applies to all of us, irrespective of age, gender, social background or professional experience and skills.

All these efforts are sensible given the more dangerous world NATO member states have to live in today and they assuredly go in the right direction. But are they sufficient to produce a true and comprehensive strategy for civilian mobilisation and resilience? Is the potential of civilians to defend their country as shown in Ukraine really being exploited to the full in all the NATO member states as the alliance rebuilds its military capabilities and ramps up defence production and planning? Clearly there is an urgent need for a comprehensive and thoroughly researched study of this question to give us a more complete insight into the current state of affairs, rather than excellent but limited or isolated individual examples. There are a number of key questions here to which policymakers and analysts are seeking answers. What are the roles and responsibilities of the various levels of authority: local (mayors and cities), national governments and their agencies such as police, border forces and emergency management departments, and multilateral international bodies like NATO and the EU? How can they work more effectively together? How can allies be incentivised to place greater emphasis on civilian resilience in their national security and defence strategies and resources and training priorities? How easily can good examples within NATO be translated elsewhere so that all allies in the future at least meet minimum standards and benchmarks in making their citizens more prepared, aware and resilient? What are the lessons of efforts across the alliance in this area over the past few decades and are they being learned, and more importantly applied?

It is the enormous merit of this book, The Eighth Baseline Requirement: Resilient Civilians in Hybrid Threats and Warfare, to provide that full and comprehensive analysis across a number of NATO countries and beyond that has been so necessary to take the debate forward. The book, the result of a research grant from the NATO Science for Peace and Security programme, has brought together the right mix (and balance) of academic experts, their rigorous research, and former practitioners, with their inside knowledge and perspectives. The two principal project directors, Gunhild Hoogensen Gjorv and Sergii Glebov, together with co-editor and project partner Christopher Holsek, have usefully complemented their own contributions by bringing in other experts with particular country area or subject matter expertise. The case histories provide valuable insights in a deep dive into Afghanistan, Ukraine or France, while the more cross-cutting chapters provide further context and help to identify general themes and objectives. The publication is also usefully timed to bring expertise and analysis to a NATO and EU wide debate that is now getting underway in Europe in the wake of NATO’s latest Strategic Concept of 2022 which states that “resilience is a national responsibility and a collective commitment” and the Alliance’s introduction of measurable Resilience capability targets into its defence planning process. The EU has also emphasised resilience in its own plans for REARM Europe and in the recently published report tasked by the Commission for European leaders on Resilience which was authored by the former Finnish President, Sauli Niinisto. Readers of this book will not only appreciate the in-depth research and high quality analysis in the pages that follow but also a style of clarity and good writing that does not always characterise academic publications, but makes this book a noteworthy exception in being both strategic and policy-oriented in its approach and engaging to read for the resilience specialist in a government ministry or think tank and the general reader alike. Understandably even such a comprehensive study as this one cannot cover every aspect of the issue or provide detailed policy recommendations across the board. But the basis of successful policy making is asking the right questions and providing accurate, up to date research data as well as simultaneously producing a myriad of insights and lessons learned from individual cases and past experiences that can illuminate the path that policymakers need to follow. This book ticks all the right boxes here. Writers of Forewords usually conclude by hoping that a book on a topical and important societal issue will have the attention that it deserves. But I will go one step further and express my full confidence that this will be the case for The Eighth Baseline Requirement: Resilient Civilians in Hybrid Threats and Warfare.

Jamie Shea

Former Deputy Assistant Secretary General of NATO for Emerging Security Challenges.

Senior Fellow, Friends of Europe, Brussels.

**Abstract**

The evolving nature of hybrid threats and grey zone conflict demonstrates that resilience cannot be secured by infrastructure and institutions alone. The past decade has made clear that hostile actors do not merely target bridges, power grids, or ministries — they increasingly target the trust, cohesion, and psychological resilience of civilian populations themselves. Disinformation campaigns, influence operations, and other non-military tactics deliberately exploit social fault lines, degrade trust in authorities, and polarize communities. When trust erodes, the effectiveness of the other seven baseline requirements is undermined. “Resilient Civilians” should be formally recognized as an eighth Baseline Requirement for National Resilience — a cross-cutting foundation that emphasizes investing in public trust, media literacy, community cohesion, and civic education. Such recognition would signal that resilience is not only about hardened infrastructure and secure supply chains but also about the will, knowledge, and cohesion of the people. This chapter introduces a proposed eighth baseline requirement in light of increasing hybrid threat activities and the need for more creative approaches to resilience. The chapter concludes with an summary of the chapters that contribute to The Eighth Baseline Requirement: Resilient Civilians in Hybrid Threats and Warfare.

[Afghanistan War, the Taliban, and Civilians: An Analysis of How Common People Shape and Alter a Conflict](/volumearticle/75175)

Authors

Arsalan Bilal

Pages

13 - 34

DOI

10.3233/NHSDP250004

Category

Research Article

**Abstract**

The policy and academic scholarship on the post-9/11 conflict in Afghanistan has unveiled a multitude of reasons for the failure of the international intervention that allowed the spread of insurgency and return of the Taliban in 2021. Nevertheless, one factor that has been understudied is the role of the civilians—or the ordinary people—in shaping the conflict dynamics in the country for two decades. The civilians are often analysed as either victims or peacemakers within a conflict. While it is true that they usually bear the brunt of violence, because of which it is intrinsically in their interest to nurture peace, their role in conflicts is multi-faceted and much more complex in nature. Focusing on the case of Afghanistan, I argue that the ordinary civilians, willingly and inadvertently, can compound and complicate a conflict. The idea is not to establish culpability of the common people but rather look critically at the Afghan conflict or the Taliban militancy in relation to ordinary civilians on the ground. I look at the spread and rise of the Taliban between 2001 and 2021 vis-à-vis sympathetic and positive attitudes towards the group at the societal level as well as neutrality on part of some people. These factors contributed towards creating an enabling environment for the Taliban to survive and thrive. Nevertheless, I deem these a function of core failures of the state-building project in Afghanistan that eroded legitimacy of the government and judicial organs. This convinced many people to view the Taliban as the lesser evil. As a corollary, the Taliban gained strength and the conflict permeated virtually all parts of the country until the international forces withdrew from the country.

**Abstract**

As diplomatic and strategic relations between China and the West continue to be contested, especially as Beijing re-emerges from its 2020–23 pandemic isolation, the Xi Jinping regime is now redefining many aspects of Chinese security. There has been a great emphasis on observation and control, a greater willingness to push back against perceived overreach by the West, and a blending of local and international security policy as well as the roles of military and civilian actors. Beijing has been more willing to combine traditional hard power with economic and informational tactics to improve its strategic standing to combat perceived US-led attempts to undermine the Chinese party-state and contain the country’s growing power. This multifaceted approach to hybrid warfare, stretching from local communities to the heights of the international system, must be understood to develop a more complete understanding of Chinese security interests and how the country is contributing to the evolution of hybrid conflict.

[Understanding Hybrid Threats and Civilian Resilience: A Case Study of France](/volumearticle/75177)

Authors

Jane Freedman

Pages

51 - 61

DOI

10.3233/NHSDP250006

Category

Research Article

**Abstract**

This paper explores hybrid threats and resilience to them through a case study of France. One of the ways in which hybrid threats have been argued to operate is through the exacerbation of existing “fault lines” in societies through disinformation, “fake news”, political influencing and support for populist movements and parties. This paper explores how this can operate in France, where some actors have used issues such as immigration and refugees to create social divisions and exploit perceived threats to French security and to secular and republican ideologies. External support for far-right political parties or, more recently, for the gilets jaunes movement, has contributed to these attempts to promote populism and widen divisions in French society. Having assessed the ways in which such hybrid threats can operate, the paper then discusses ways in which the threat of disinformation to national cohesion and security might be mitigated.

**Abstract**

The full-scale military invasion of Ukraine in 2022 opened the final stage of Russia’s hybrid aggression against the Ukrainian state. This phase has been marked by a terrorist-like war that targets both military and civilians. The following chapter investigates the links between the hybrid threats posed by ‘new generation warfare’, the Russian full-fledged invasion of Ukraine, and the genesis of Ukraine’s resilience during the First Hybrid World War. The latter refers to Russia’s long-term policy of confrontation with the West as its geopolitical alter ego. The Russian dictator’s war in Ukraine revealed and inaugurated a much more far-reaching Kremlin’s protracted chaos-making strategy, one that moves from a values-based world order toward a crisis-oriented one. Ukraine’s resilience confronts the Kremlin’s destructive course. The focus of this chapter is on the phenomenon of Ukraine’s national resilience and resistance as the cornerstones of its national defense system, which emerged throughout Ukraine’s ongoing liberation war against Russian aggressors.

[Warning and Responding to Hybrid Threats: Between Theory and Practice](/volumearticle/75181)

Authors

Sebastiaan Rietjens

Pages

87 - 92

DOI

10.3233/NHSDP250010

Category

Research Article

**Abstract**

If a society knows what threats to expect, in terms of e.g. size, impact and origin, it becomes better able to anticipate and focus its activities to counter these threats. This makes warning a crucial element in building resilience. Hybrid threats however, are wicked problems: They are ambiguous and fuzzy, and there is no proven knowledge or fixed standards for adequately addressing them. As a result, warning about hybrid threats is intrinsically difficult and challenging. This chapter explores how the warning process, including communicating warnings in a timely manner, takes place in the context of hybrid conflicts. The paper draws upon insights from the strategic warning and early warning literatures as well as the literature on complexity theory. The paper discusses four phases in the warning process: (1) direction of the warning efforts, (2) collecting the data, (3) analyzing the threats, and (4) communicating the warning analyses. For each phase, the paper analyses the challenges that confront warning for hybrid threats. These challenges include the warning-response gap, Western centrism, the selection of indicators, and reliance on single methods. This analysis will allow policymakers and other actors to better cope with hybrid threats.

[Targeting Fears, Prejudices, and Differing Values: Identity and Hybrid Threats](/volumearticle/75182)

Authors

Outi Jalonen, Velomahanina Razakamaharavo, Jane Freedman, Gunhild Hoogensen Gjorv

Pages

DOI

10.3233/NHSDP250011

Category

**Abstract**

Democratic societies and states rely on trust – both between civilians and their governing authorities, as well as between civilians themselves, as neighbours and fellow-members of society. Formenting division between people by targeting existing vulnerabilities and weaknesses in society has increasingly become standard practice for state and non-state actors interested in weakening a state without resorting to traditional military means and the use of force. Identity markers like gender and gender identification, class, race, ethnicity, age, and sexual orientation are targeted in multiple ways, both to cause harm to marginalized groups who do not conform to traditional and/or dominant group identity markes, but also to increase anger, hate, fear, and disgust among those who harbour existing uncertainties and/or prejudices towards certain segments of the population. This chapter addresses the ways in which identity is used, particularly in disinformation campaigns, in attempts to impact and fragment democratic societies. It also examines the role of civilians in resisting such campaigns as a part of civilian resilience.

**Abstract**

How would the civilian population of a specific country, as distinct from the various layers of government or organized civil defence, respond to significant disruptions caused by hybrid threats? This chapter explores different response scenarios and considers what can be done to strengthen the resilience and adaptive capacities of a civilian population, and its social institutions, when such threats are a likely risk. The chapter explains how systems dynamics, non-linearity and self-organization combine and interact to generate complexity, and what the implications are for how civilian populations and social institutions are likely to respond to hybrid threats, where the centre of gravity is social cohesion and public trust, and where one of the main challenges is the uncertainty and unpredictability of both the threat and how people will respond to it. Faced with this kind of problem set, the chapter recommends utilizing an adaptive approach that is designed to cope with the complexity, uncertainty and unpredictability one encounters when attempting to influence complex social systems.

[Community Service in Civil Defense Strategies of Resilience](/volumearticle/75184)

Authors

Christopher Holshek

Pages

108 - 115

DOI

10.3233/NHSDP250013

Category

Research Article

**Abstract**

Civil society is the strategic center of gravity of 21st century peace and security. Hybrid, cognitive and other gray-zone warfare aim to cripple governance, social and political cohesion, and civilian resilience, attacking the social trust that is the glue of democratic civil society. Civil defence and preparedness need reframing as a strategy of resilience. Beyond protecting physical infrastructure, they must empower citizens as the first and best line of defense against cognitive threats, promoting strategic narratives about citizenship as participatory governance and service as a rite of passage for inclusion, if not leadership, in democratic civil society. From a security and stability standpoint, civic engagement and community service comprise a form of civil defense, mitigating societal vulnerabilities and political weaknesses by developing capacities for civil resilience and social cohesion at the most fundamental levels of social organization and collective identity formulation, along the same self-paced bottom-up capacity development lines as adaptive peacebuilding. Especially since the COVID-19 outbreak, the value of service as a unifying as well as democratizing narrative has gathered strength in North Atlantic societies. Public and higher education schools can help implement a strategy of resilience as incubators of democratic citizenship, civilian resilience, and social cohesion. NATO would do well to incorporate civic engagement and community service in revised concepts of civil defense and preparedness as well as resilience, integrating them into its Comprehensive Approach, Civil-Military Interaction policy, and Civil Military Cooperation doctrine, and helping member and partner states socialize these concepts as nationally and culturally appropriate. Such resilient strategies can flip what has been a societal source of weakness into a source of strength and a civil defence and preparedness strategy of resilience into an offensive strategy of playing strengths rather than simply responding to threats.

**Abstract**

In this chapter the authors revisit the concept of hybrid threats and their role in targeting existing vulnerabilities in democracies, not least brought on by increasing dissatisfaction or sense of marginalization within parts of democratic popuations, in an attempt to weaken and destabilize them. The authors additionally draw on developments in Norwegian total defence and total preparedness policy, identifying the ways in which Norway has developed total preparedness thinking in response to the increased targeting of civilians by hybrid threat activities, as well as responding to the clear demands being made on individual civilians to withstand crisis or war. The authors thereafter review some of the key assumptions and measures that the recent 2025 Total Preparedness White Paper offers, highlighting further recommendations that can strengthen the roles of resilient civilians.
